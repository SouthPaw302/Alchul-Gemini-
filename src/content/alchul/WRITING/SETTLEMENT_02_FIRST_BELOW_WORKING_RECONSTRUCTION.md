# The First Below — Working Reconstruction of a Spring-Margin Habitat

**Status:** working fictional reconstruction, **not locked canon**. Corresponding real-science analysis: `RESEARCH/FIRST_BELOW.md`. Read that file first.

**Relative placement:** after the surface civilization has mastered large-scale environmental management, but before its later society begins the civilization-wide Long Descent. This is an early continuously occupied *frontier*, not a refuge built after the final K–Pg warning.

**Provisional name:** The Spring Margin. It is a descriptive working label, not an approved translation from the inhabitants' language.

## 1. Where the frontier begins

The survey party leaves a surface settlement of the Braided Reach kind along a route maintained across many generations. The land rises or firms toward older carbonate and fractured bedrock. Their world has no modern political boundary and its watercourses must not be copied from today's Missouri.

They do not discover an empty underground continent.

They locate several unreliable cavities associated with a spring system: an open-air seep where water has already traveled through stone; a low chamber reached from a higher dry margin; wet passages beyond which the party cannot see; a high fracture that sometimes carries air; and old collapse rubble that proves the ground cannot always be trusted. Some portions are connected by water and pressure, but not by traversable space.

They find places that can be studied. Only much later will they make one of them a place to live.

In this reconstruction, access to the first occupied chamber stays above the normal water table and away from the most unstable roof. Its exact dimension, depth, latitude, and geological unit remain unresolved. We do not call it a cavern city because it is not one.

The original reason for repeated visits is not an abstract desire to live underground. The springs reveal material behavior invisible at the surface: chemically altered stone, water that responds to distant rainfall, small organisms growing where sunlight cannot enter, and vibrations that reach a trained body through rock. The frontier offers an observatory, a laboratory, and a secure duplicate store.

## 2. The first survey takes seasons

The party marks the changes of a full wet and dry cycle, then another. Individuals with fine geological sensitivity record what the site does before a storm, during a storm, and after pressure returns to normal. They read the rock's stress, flow, and vibration, but they do not move stone by intention alone.

A chamber that seems safe during dry weather fills with cold air during a different season. An apparently sheltered route floods when water backs up through a lower conduit. One opening proves to be a poor exhaust because air reverses at an inconvenient temperature transition.

The rejected chambers are remembered. Rejection is a technical accomplishment: the society knows more about where *not* to raise its young.

Small interventions follow:
- clear a narrow drainage channel at a known natural low point;
- reinforce a loose surface by targeted wedges and selected material;
- divide the spring into an upstream intake and a separate work-water route;
- create removable air screens and protected exhaust openings;
- keep a high path passable under flooding;
- establish a cache at the surface that can support an emergency withdrawal.

All of these interventions require work, replacement, and maintenance. The site is not yet self-healing.

## 3. Habitation is assembled as interdependent systems

The first permanent layout is not a series of neat human rooms. It is a set of functions arranged around air, water, body movement, and access.

### Threshold and exchange ground

Near the main entrance, loads arriving from the surface are checked, cleaned, divided, and stored. Wet material is not blindly admitted to the deepest chambers. Return expeditions take spent containers, waste that cannot be safely processed, and damaged equipment back toward the surface. There is a receiving area where unfamiliar cultures can be isolated.

### The breathing way

A broad safe passage connects the outer entrance with the habitation space. At least one separate high-flow path is maintained so air can exchange without requiring all traffic to move through the same opening. Carefully shaped constrictions and screens moderate drafts; they are adjusted as the season changes. Workers check the atmospheric response instead of assuming a fixed flow direction. Certain foul-smelling or biologically active work zones have independent air paths and remain unoccupied until tested.

### The water margin

Spring water passes through a protected intake and a settlement-visible inspection point, then into different paths for consumption, controlled cultivation, work, and discharge. Waste flows away from the source and from occupied space. A reserve remains available when the spring is turbid or chemically abnormal. The inhabitants know that an apparently clean underground stream may carry something from far outside their territory.

### The dwelling shelves

Habitation occupies stable, high, dry-enough rock shelves and cultivated support surfaces near the good air path. Paths are curving and locally broad because the body plan is theropod-derived, modified over millions of years, and not yet pinned to a specific final morphology. Where some inhabitants still need tail clearance, the routes accommodate it. Movement is tactile as well as visual: the route announces itself through surface, sound, scent, air motion, and remembered spatial sequence.

### The nursery

The protected reproductive area is nearer to clean air, reliable food transfer, stable heat, and the safest departure route than the specialists' most interesting underground laboratory. By this historical period, egg-bearing individuals retain developing eggs internally for approximately three quarters of development. The last external quarter requires safe incubation. The nursery must therefore work both for the body carrying advanced embryos and for eggs nearing hatching.

Temperature is moderated by thermal mass and controlled airflow. The keepers treat oxygen, humidity, contamination, and evacuation as linked problems. No constant open flame is allowed to consume the air inside this enclosure.

### The conversion ground

Here are fermentation, preservation, selective fungal and microbial cultivation, treatment of fibers or minerals, and experiments on biological filters. The surface supplies much of the energy bound in incoming organic materials. The underground processors transform and preserve it; they do not create limitless food by calling a recycling system an ecosystem.

### The knowledge ground

At least two independent ways preserve technical lessons: maintained material patterns and supervised training, supplemented by chemical, rhythmic, and spatial records. A flood that kills a carefully selected culture is not allowed to erase its preparation method. Some apprentices learn the different sounds of a failing water way before they learn the formal sequence used to record it.

### The observation reach

A small chamber is retained for controlled observations of the water, stone, unknown microbial activity, and unusual perception associated with Alchul. Observers are not automatically priests or rulers. Their reports can be valuable, wrong, contradictory, or unable to be repeated. An anomalous vision of a dry chamber under water is a reason to inspect drainage, not a license to stop measuring it.

### Hazard pockets

Dangerous gas outlets, stagnant depressions, unstable fracture spaces, and poorly surveyed water branches are marked and separated. The settlement has some places it has deliberately chosen **not** to inhabit.

## 4. What sustains the residents

A permanent resident still eats in a way that respects their lineage's predatory history. The precise peak-era diet is unresolved, but the station receives prepared food, preserved animal-derived resources, usable plant or fungal substrates, and selected living cultures from the surface. Some resident tasks involve experimentation with underground biomass production and resource conversion. The first generations do not subsist on imaginary endless luminous fungi.

A recurring supply journey delivers more than calories. It brings living breeding lines and microbial cultures, technical specialists, replacement materials, seasonal information, and contact with the surface. Its return journeys carry failed trials, observations, preserved samples, and people.

This two-way exchange changes the meaning of the station. A surface household that has never visited the cave may depend on a chemistry learned there. A young underground inhabitant may depend on a distant surface harvest to live.

At first, the station remains an elaborate extension of the civilization above. Only persistence turns it into something with its own center of gravity.

## 5. The first permanent residents

No exact population has been established. An initial continuously occupied group should be small enough that its needs can be measured and large enough to survive the loss of a specialist. This is a *criterion*, not a number.

The residents are chosen for overlapping competencies, not a single hierarchy:
- reading water and rock;
- monitoring air and heat;
- sustaining cultivated chemical and biological systems;
- preserving and distributing food;
- guarding development and rearing young;
- maintaining routes to the surface;
- testing observations and teaching what has failed.

There are disputes. Some dislike the underground because every mistake can affect everyone else's breath. Others value its quiet stability and its unusual informational richness. Some worry the distant surface partners will decide the supply priorities; some surface residents resent the investment in an inhospitable place.

The first children who complete their incubation below are not necessarily a triumphal new species. They simply make clear that the habitat has become a social home rather than a scientific appointment. Their learning grounds and paths are altered because their own movement and sensory experience differ from the adults' expectations.

Some descendants will know the open sky personally. Others, much later, may know it from instruction and inherited experience alone.

## 6. The first serious system test

This is a proposed engineering incident, **not** an established chapter in the history.

An interval of unusual outside warmth changes the relative pressures in the cave. One of the secondary ventilation paths slows, then changes direction. At the same time, a distant rain pulse alters the spring, bringing sediment toward a water screen. The inhabitants notice not one spectacular disaster but several small failures that interact.

A fermentation-ground caretaker reports that a culture has changed odor. An air tender reports an unfamiliar flow. A nursery attendant notices that an egg-bearer is tiring under conditions that would not yet alarm a healthy active scout.

No amount of expertise can make the missing airflow reappear by wishing.

They isolate the productive but biologically risky chamber, open the alternate air path, direct a limited crew to the blocked water screen, and reduce energy use and occupancy in the affected inner zone. Surface runners are sent to the high cache. The nursery is prepared to relocate along a route already tested for its least mobile members.

The station survives, but the event proves that its 'secondary' ventilation path was not independent enough. Its plans are revised. The next season's settlement is a different machine from the previous season's settlement.

We should not declare that Alchul predicted or solved the event. One observer may have experienced something uncanny; the technical decisions still need observable physical causes and reproducible outcomes.

## 7. What counts as the founding achievement

The first below-ground permanent habitat does **not** prove that the civilization can outlive the coming mass extinction. No one at this stage necessarily knows a planetary catastrophe is coming.

It proves narrower, extraordinary things:

1. A population can continuously occupy managed darkness without treating every visit as an expedition.
2. Air, water, heat, chemistry, food, and reproductive care can function as a coupled system.
3. The inhabitants can recover from one or more local failures without losing all stored learning.
4. A young generation can grow, observe, and learn below.
5. The first habitat can act as a template from which another different habitat might be made.

The real breakthrough is a repeatable method, not a giant hall.

## 8. How the frontier becomes an inhabited network

Once the method exists, communities do not need to copy the first station's layout. A different spring requires a different air geometry. A rock fracture can carry useful water yet be unsuitable for egg development. A shallow chamber may serve as an archive and medicine store while a distant, better-ventilated node becomes a permanent nursery.

Surface and subsurface systems become economically entwined. Knowledge and organisms are deliberately duplicated at distant points, especially after floods, failures of fermentation cultures, and loss of specialist lineages. Early trails become maintained transfer corridors. Geology provides the raw inequalities, the cracks, water and heat gradients; the civilization slowly makes those inequalities into infrastructure.

Much later, an already mature deep network enables the civilizational relocation described in `LAST_SURFACE_AGE.md`.

**This is not yet that final withdrawal.**

## 9. What a scientifically honest image or simulation should show

If this stage is depicted later, useful visual evidence would be:
- a small uneven natural chamber above water level, not a cathedral-scale cavern;
- an active spring and flood channels, with clear safe and unsafe margins;
- a visible second air path and field-maintained screens;
- high, protected incubation places and wide movement paths;
- subdued/local illumination with areas deliberately dark;
- surface loads and a connected escape route;
- material that seems grown, tended, braced, eroded, and repaired rather than uniformly manufactured.

Avoid a modern mine, symmetrical stone palace, metallic cyberpunk hall, prehistoric humans, robed reptilian people, giant glowing mushroom city, unexplained giant equipment, or omnipresent light.

## 10. Canon audit and open items

**Preserved existing canon:**
- The founders are Late Cretaceous terrestrial Appalachian theropod-derived predators.
- The first durable underground systems come *after* sophisticated surface culture.
- Geological sensing begins as detection of stress, pressure, water, heat and fracture, not telekinesis.
- The 75%-internal egg-development premise holds for the peak surface stage.
- Alchul's nature is unresolved and its influence never replaces engineering.
- The luminous deep world is substantially cultivated and engineered later.
- Surface and underground populations coexist for a long time before K–Pg.

**All of the following remain proposals:**
- The label Spring Margin; its precise bedrock, location, cave entrance and extent;
- chamber plan, independent vents, reserve intake and first crisis;
- occupations, site size, duration of the earliest occupation, and demographic composition;
- whether this particular settlement is ancestral to a given modern deep city;
- whether any of its named ecological cultures survives the later catastrophe;
- exact developmental anatomy and nursery methods.

**Next research checkpoint:** choose and verify a specific Maastrichtian-compatible corridor of carbonate outcrops/karst at the northern embayment edge, then build a constrained section drawing with water table, vents, flood plane, safe elevated shelter, and surface resupply. Only after this should a named first-below settlement enter locked canon.
